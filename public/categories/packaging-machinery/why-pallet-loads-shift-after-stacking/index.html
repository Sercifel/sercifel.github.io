Why Pallet Loads Shift After Stacking: Layer Patterns, Product Rigidity, and Conveyor Handoffs

Aug 25, 2026

A pallet can look square at the end of a palletizing cycle and still begin to lean, telescope, or creep minutes later. Stability is not determined by placement accuracy alone. A finished unit load is a mechanical system: cases, bags, bundles, stretch film, and the pallet all contribute stiffness, friction, and movement.

Three factors explain many post-stacking shifts: layer pattern, product rigidity, and conveyor handoffs. Understanding how they interact helps separate a palletizer-placement problem from a broader unit-load design issue.

Layer Patterns Create Different Force Paths

Columnar stacking places cases directly above one another so their corners and vertical panels remain aligned. This generally supports vertical compression because strong structural areas carry the load.

Interlocked patterns rotate or offset adjacent layers so packages overlap. They can improve geometric cohesion, but upper cases may load the panels of lower cases rather than their strongest edges. Fibre Box Association research conducted with Rochester Institute of Technology found that, for the corrugated cases tested, aligned column stacking retained substantially more compression strength than interlocked or hybrid arrangements. Deliberate misalignment also reduced compression performance.

Pattern Typical Advantage Main Tradeoff
Columnar Strong vertical load path Layers may slide if friction or containment is weak
Interlocked More overlap between layers Often lower corrugated-case compression strength
Hybrid Balances overlap and alignment Results depend on geometry and transition layers

No pattern is universally best. A rigid carton may perform well in columns, while a tall or narrow package may need more lateral interlock. The choice depends on case dimensions, pallet footprint, product weight, surface friction, load height, and containment.

Product Rigidity Changes How Errors Accumulate

Two loads with identical dimensions can behave differently if their packages have different stiffness.

Rigid cartons, crates, or tightly packed cases usually preserve their shape. That helps maintain a defined pattern, but rigid layers can also transmit acceleration with little deformation. If friction between layers is low, sections of the load may slide as relatively solid blocks.

Flexible products behave differently. Bags, lightweight shrink bundles, thin-wall bottles, or partially filled containers can deform under vertical load. Deformation may increase contact area, but it can also create bulging, leaning, or uneven surfaces. Once a layer is no longer flat, the next layer sits on a less predictable base.

Corrugated packaging adds an environmental variable. PMMI and the Fibre Box Association note that high humidity can adversely affect corrugated-board performance. A pattern that performs well with dry cases may therefore behave differently after humid storage.

Rigidity is best treated as a system property. Package stiffness, internal support, case compression strength, fill level, closure method, and surface material all influence whether a layer resists motion or gradually changes shape.

Conveyor Handoffs Can Start the Shift

A finished pallet may travel from the palletizer to a wrapper, then to accumulation and forklift pickup. Each transfer changes velocity.

A controlled transfer keeps those changes small. A problematic one may include a gap, height mismatch, abrupt stop, roller-speed mismatch, or hard restart. The pallet base reacts first, while the upper mass tends to keep moving because of inertia. That creates a short shear force through the load. If friction and containment are insufficient, layers can move slightly. Repeated transfers can turn small movements into visible lean or telescoping.

The issue can also begin before pallet placement. Cases feeding the palletizer may arrive skewed, compressed together, or inconsistently spaced. The palletizer can place them accurately relative to what it receives, yet the layer may already contain uneven contact forces.

Mitsubishi Heavy Industries describes stable, gentle handling as important for transfer equipment. ISTA research has documented lateral movement between layers of unitized bottled-water loads during multi-axis testing and field transport, showing that load behavior can differ from what is seen under purely vertical vibration.

Troubleshooting the Whole Unit Load

When a load shifts, changing palletizer settings first may hide the real cause. Instead, observe the sequence from infeed and layer formation through placement, discharge, wrapping, accumulation, and transport.

If distortion appears before wrapping, review layer geometry, case orientation, conveyor pressure, and placement timing. If movement starts during pallet transfer, examine conveyor elevations, acceleration, gaps, and stopping positions. If the load remains square through the cell but shifts later, containment force, pallet friction, package deformation, and distribution hazards become stronger suspects.

Testing can help validate changes. ISTA includes procedures for unitized loads, including Procedure 1E for integrity screening and Procedure 3E for similar packaged products in truckload shipment. ASTM D4169 provides a broader framework for evaluating shipping units against anticipated distribution hazards.

FAQ

Why does a pallet shift even when every case is placed accurately?

Placement accuracy controls initial geometry, not later forces. Friction, deformation, conveyor acceleration, stretch-film behavior, and transport vibration can still move layers.

Is interlocked stacking always more stable than column stacking?

No. Interlocking may improve lateral cohesion, but it can reduce vertical compression strength in corrugated loads. The better pattern depends on geometry, stiffness, friction, and containment.

Can stretch wrap correct a poor pallet pattern?

It can improve containment, but it should not be expected to compensate for severe misalignment, weak packages, overhang, or harsh transfers. ISTA work shows that wrapping method can influence lateral sway.

Why do flexible packages shift over time?

They can creep, bulge, or settle under sustained compression. As geometry changes, contact surfaces and the load's center of mass may change too.

What should be checked first when a load starts leaning?

Find the first point where lean appears: after stacking, discharge, wrapping, or accumulation. That usually narrows the cause quickly and helps avoid unnecessary equipment adjustments.

Should pallet stability be tested before changing equipment settings?

Where practical, yes. Controlled testing helps distinguish packaging limitations from machine-control problems and prevents one adjustment from masking another.

Conclusion

Post-stacking load shift is rarely caused by one variable. Layer patterns determine how forces travel through the stack, product rigidity affects whether packages hold their geometry, and conveyor handoffs introduce dynamic events that can start or amplify movement.

The most effective improvement process is system-based: identify where motion begins, verify the package and layer design, reduce unnecessary transfer shocks, and validate the complete unit load under realistic handling conditions.
