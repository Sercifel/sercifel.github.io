# Aerosol Can Manufacturing Lines: Can Making Machine Technology and Safety Standards

![](https://www.shinican.com/web/image/7990-2cdb9ca9/Aerosol-can01.png)

An aerosol can looks simple from the outside: a cylindrical steel body, a domed top, a valve, and a curled base. What happens inside a production facility to get there is anything but simple. Unlike a standard food or beverage can, an aerosol can has to hold internal pressure for its entire shelf life, survive temperature swings during shipping, and pass regulatory testing before it can legally cross a border. Personal care products, household chemicals, automotive aftermarket goods, industrial coatings, and food items such as whipped cream and cooking spray each put a different chemical load and pressure profile inside the same basic steel or aluminum shell, which is part of why aerosol manufacturing tolerances run tighter than for cans built to hold food or beverages at atmospheric pressure. For buyers evaluating a can making machine line for aerosol production, understanding the forming process, the coating requirements, and the pressure standards that govern the finished container is the difference between a smooth vendor selection and an expensive retrofit six months later. This article walks through how aerosol cans are actually made, what safety standards apply, and what to check before committing to a production line.

## Why Aerosol Cans Require a Different Manufacturing Approach

Most metal aerosol cans in production today are three-piece steel construction: a welded cylindrical body, a bottom end, and a top end (or mounting cup) that accepts the valve. Two-piece aluminum aerosol cans, drawn and ironed from a single slug with no side seam, are common in cosmetics and personal care where a seamless finish is preferred. The equipment used to produce either format shares a lineage with broader industrial canning machine platforms, since the core disciplines, sheet handling, precision forming, seam integrity, and coating control, carry over between product categories even though the end use and pressure requirements differ substantially. What sets an aerosol line apart is that every one of those disciplines has to be executed to a tighter tolerance, because the finished can will be pressurized and shipped, not just filled and sealed at atmospheric pressure.

Demand for aerosol packaging has also pushed can makers to support faster changeovers between can diameters and heights, since brand owners increasingly request smaller production runs across more SKUs. That shift matters for anyone comparing equipment, because a line built only for long, single-format runs will struggle to compete on total cost once changeover frequency rises.

## How Forming, Coating, and Seaming Each Determine Can Safety

An aerosol can body begins as flat tinplate or aluminum sheet, cut to a blank and rolled into a cylinder. For three-piece steel cans, the side seam is closed by electric resistance welding, which fuses the overlapping edges of the sheet without added filler material. The weld seam is then typically covered with a protective stripe of lacquer or powder coating, applied and cured in-line, so that the exposed steel at the weld does not become a corrosion point once the can is filled.

Interior coating is arguably the most safety-critical step in the entire process, and it is often underestimated by buyers who focus mostly on forming speed. Aerosol formulations, particularly those with solvents, propellants, or acidic ingredients, can corrode bare steel or aluminum from the inside out, which weakens the can wall and raises the risk of pressure failure over time. Interior coatings (commonly epoxy-phenolic or organosol systems) are sprayed or applied by roller and then cured in an oven at a controlled temperature and dwell time. Coating thickness and cure quality are typically verified with conductivity testing (sometimes called an "enamel rater" or holiday test), which checks for pinholes or thin spots that would expose bare metal to the product inside.

Once the body is formed and coated, the ends are attached by seaming, a mechanical folding process that interlocks the flange of the can body with the flange of the end under controlled rollers, rather than adhesive or welding. For most three-piece aerosol cans, the bottom end is double-seamed onto the body at the can maker. The top of the can is necked and flanged inward to a smaller diameter to accept the valve mounting cup, and after the valve is inserted and the can is filled with product and propellant, the mounting cup is seamed onto the neck, in many aerosol formats using a triple seam rather than the double seam standard on food and beverage cans, to add an extra fold of material at the highest-stress point of a pressurized container. Seam quality is checked by cutting a sample seam cross-section and measuring seam thickness, overlap, and tightness against the can specification, a process that quality teams typically repeat at set intervals throughout a production run, not just at startup.

Before an aerosol can is approved for use, it goes through pressure and leak testing. Industry practice, reflected in aerosol packaging standards used across North America and echoed in international frameworks referenced by the UN Model Regulations for the Transport of Dangerous Goods, calls for finished cans to withstand internal pressure well beyond what they will experience in normal storage and transport, commonly framed as a multiple of the pressure the product generates at an elevated reference temperature such as 130°F (54°C). Cans are also run through a hot water bath test at that reference temperature to check for leaks, seam failure, or visible distortion before they are released to production. Buyers should be careful to separate what is a hard regulatory requirement (such as UN packaging classification and transport testing for the finished, filled product) from what is internal manufacturing best practice (such as in-line leak detection stations or statistical sampling plans), since the two are related but not identical, and compliance responsibility for the filled, labeled product ultimately sits with the filler and brand owner, not solely with the can maker.

## Evaluating an Aerosol Can Making Machine Line: What Buyers Should Check

Choosing an aerosol can making machine line is not just a matter of comparing rated speed in cans per minute. Because the finished product has to pass pressure and leak testing downstream, the forming and seaming precision built into the machine has a direct effect on scrap rates and compliance risk later in the supply chain. A few areas are worth walking through carefully with any prospective supplier:

- **Seam consistency across the full run, not just at startup.** Ask for seam thickness and overlap data from a full production shift, not a single sample pull, since seaming rollers wear and drift over time.
- **Changeover time between can diameters and heights.** If the product line requires multiple SKUs, tooling changeover speed affects real-world output more than peak rated speed does.
- **Interior coating and cure verification method.** Confirm what in-line testing (such as conductivity or holiday testing) is built into the line versus what has to be done manually offline.
- **Documentation trail.** Request sample mill certificates for tinplate or aluminum stock, weld inspection records, and seam test logs, since these documents are what a filler or regulator will ask for if a batch is later questioned.
- **Compatibility with triple-seam tooling** if the product line includes higher-pressure formulations, since not every can body line is set up to run triple seams on the mounting cup without a tooling change.

The table below summarizes the main construction differences buyers evaluate when deciding between three-piece and two-piece aerosol can formats, since the choice affects both the machinery required and the downstream testing profile.

| Feature | Three-piece steel aerosol can | Two-piece aluminum aerosol can |
|---|---|---|
| Body construction | Welded cylindrical body plus two ends | Drawn and ironed, seamless body |
| Typical seaming points | Bottom double seam, top seam at valve cup | Single seam at valve cup only |
| Common end use | Household, industrial, automotive aerosols | Cosmetics, personal care, premium finishes |
| Side seam inspection | Weld and coating stripe integrity check | Not applicable (no side seam) |

Neither format is universally "better." The right choice depends on the fill product's compatibility with steel or aluminum, target pressure class, cosmetic finish requirements, and existing filling line infrastructure, which is why this decision is usually made jointly with a filler or co-packer rather than by the can maker alone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What pressure do aerosol cans need to withstand before they are approved for use?**
A: There is no single universal number, because the required test pressure depends on the product's internal pressure at the reference temperature used for testing, typically 130°F (54°C) in commonly referenced frameworks. What is consistent across most standards is the underlying principle: the can must not rupture at a pressure meaningfully below what it would experience at that elevated reference temperature, giving a built-in safety margin above worst-case storage and transport conditions.

**Q: Is a can making machine line itself subject to DOT or UN certification?**
A: No. Transport classification and testing (such as UN packaging designations referenced in dangerous goods regulations, or DOT-aligned specifications used in North America) apply to the finished, filled, and labeled aerosol product, not to the manufacturing equipment. A can making machine line needs to be capable of consistently producing cans that can pass that downstream testing, but the certification itself sits with the filled product and the party responsible for placing it on the market.

**Q: Can the same production line run both food-grade cans and aerosol cans?**
A: Some core equipment, such as sheet feeding, body forming, and seaming stations, can be shared or adapted across food canning machine and aerosol can making machine lines, since both rely on similar forming and seaming mechanics. However, aerosol production typically requires additional necking, flanging, and triple-seam capability for the valve end, along with interior coating systems suited to the specific chemical resistance the aerosol formulation demands, so a line has to be specified with aerosol requirements in mind from the start rather than adapted as an afterthought.

## Conclusion

Aerosol can manufacturing sits at the intersection of metal forming precision and pressure safety engineering. The body has to be welded or drawn cleanly, the interior coating has to hold up against the specific chemistry of the product it will contain, and every seam, particularly the one that closes around the valve, has to be built to handle sustained internal pressure rather than the atmospheric conditions a standard food can experiences. Buyers who treat pressure testing and seam documentation as a checklist item late in the process tend to run into costly surprises once a filled, labeled product reaches a transport or import inspection. Building those requirements into the equipment evaluation from day one, rather than after the line is already running, is what actually keeps an aerosol packaging operation compliant and safe over the long run.
