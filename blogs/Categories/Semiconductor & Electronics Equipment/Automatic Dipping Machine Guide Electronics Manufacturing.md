# Automatic Dipping Machine Selection Guide for Electronics Manufacturing

![](https://i.meee.com.tw/GvZSWws.png)

_Choose the machine around your real product, process window, material behavior, handling method, and required output, not around a headline throughput figure._

An automatic dipping machine can improve consistency, reduce direct handling, and support higher-volume production. However, it is not a universal solution that can be selected by capacity alone. For manufacturers of passive components, ceramic chips, chip resistors, MLCCs, and ceramic inductors, the wrong equipment choice can create persistent problems: uneven termination thickness, trapped air, damaged parts, poor yield, difficult changeovers, or an expensive line that cannot meet its promised output.

The most reliable selection process starts with the part and follows the process from loading to unloading. Buyers should define the product shape, dipping requirement, paste behavior, carrier method, automation level, and expected production volume before comparing suppliers. This approach makes technical discussions clearer and helps prevent a machine specification from looking suitable on paper but failing to fit the actual factory process.

Global semiconductor sales reached $630.5 billion in 2024, according to the Semiconductor Industry Association and World Semiconductor Trade Statistics. As demand grows across communications, automotive, industrial, medical, and AI-related applications, electronics manufacturers face stronger pressure to maintain repeatable quality while scaling production. In this environment, an automatic dipping machine is not simply a stand-alone asset. It is a process-control decision.

## What Does an Automatic Dipping Machine Do?

An automatic dipping machine applies conductive or functional paste to specified areas of electronic components, often to form terminations or electrode-related structures. In passive-component production, the machine may load components into a carrier, preheat them, dip them into paste, remove bubbles, dry or cure the material, transfer the components, and unload them for the next manufacturing stage.

The value of automation is not just labor reduction. A well-matched system helps control the variables that affect the final result:

- Dipping depth and contact position
- Paste thickness and leveling
- Carrier alignment and part orientation
- Air-bubble removal
- Drying temperature and dwell time
- Transfer force during loading and unloading
- Traceable, repeatable machine settings

A process can still be unstable if the paste, carrier, part geometry, or upstream loading method is not compatible with the machine. Therefore, the selection question is not, “Which automatic dipping machine is fastest?” It is, “Which equipment configuration can consistently produce our required result?”

## Start With the Product: Size, Shape, Fragility, and Termination Design

The part itself is the first filter. A machine designed for a small rectangular chip may not be appropriate for an unusual shape, a three-terminal component, or a product that needs dipping on more than one surface.

Before requesting a quotation, prepare a product data sheet that includes:

- Part type and end-use application
- Length, width, height, and dimensional tolerances
- Minimum and maximum part sizes across the product family
- Shape: rectangular, square, I-type, diamond-type, or custom geometry
- Number and location of terminals
- Required dipped area and termination pattern
- Ceramic strength, edge sensitivity, and acceptable handling force
- Cosmetic and electrical acceptance criteria

Small parts create a narrower process window. As component size falls, a small shift in dip depth, paste level, alignment, or transfer pressure can have a larger effect on coverage and dimensional consistency. A supplier should be able to review actual samples or technical drawings, not only a component name.

For example, LONG Automatic Machinery lists its LGTM-6837 TCP Full Automatic Single Side Dipping Machine for terminal dipping of passive-component chips from 0402 inch and above. This does not mean it is automatically the right machine for every 0402 product. The paste formulation, carrier pattern, required termination geometry, and total process sequence still need engineering confirmation.

## Select the Dipping Process Before Selecting the Machine

“Dipping” describes a family of processes rather than a single method. The correct configuration depends on where material must be applied and how the component is held during the operation.

Common process requirements include:

- Single-side terminal dipping
- Double-side dipping
- Slant dipping for angled or controlled coverage
- Three-terminal component dipping
- Array-style processing
- Custom dipping for non-standard geometries

The machine must support the intended dipping shape without forcing the product into an unsuitable carrier or transfer sequence. For instance, a line that requires a controlled termination profile may need accurate leveling, stable tray flatness, and precise parallelism between the dipping plate and ink tray. A line with a recurring bubble issue may require an integrated vacuum debubble stage rather than manual corrective work after dipping.

The following table connects common process questions to equipment implications.

|Process question|Why it matters|What to confirm with the machine supplier|
|---|---|---|
|Is dipping required on one side, two sides, or multiple terminals?|The number and position of dipped surfaces determine the machine sequence and transfer method.|Supported dipping configuration, orientation control, and changeover requirements.|
|Does the product need a flat, angled, or custom termination profile?|Dipping geometry affects electrical performance, plating preparation, and appearance.|Dip-head movement, leveling method, tray flatness, and process examples using similar parts.|
|Is air entrapment a known defect risk?|Bubbles can leave voids or inconsistent paste coverage.|Availability and operating range of vacuum debubble equipment.|
|Does the paste require preheating or controlled drying?|Poor thermal handling can change paste flow, adhesion, and final thickness.|Oven temperature range, airflow or pressure design, cooling, and buffer capacity.|
|Are product sizes changed often?|Frequent changeovers can reduce actual line utilization.|Carrier changeover steps, recipe storage, tooling cost, and validation support.|

Do not accept vague statements such as “suitable for various products” as a process guarantee. Ask the supplier to explain how the machine handles your specific product at each critical stage: loading, placement, dipping, debubbling, drying, transfer, and unloading.

## Evaluate Paste and Material Behavior as a System

Paste behavior is one of the most important and most overlooked parts of equipment selection. Two manufacturers can run the same component size but require different machine settings because their paste viscosity, solids content, particle size, drying profile, or leveling behavior is different.

The objective is not to find a machine with the highest possible precision number. The objective is to establish a repeatable process window that maintains the specified termination result under normal production conditions.

Discuss these material questions with the equipment supplier and paste supplier together:

- What paste type is used, and what is its recommended operating viscosity range?
- How sensitive is the material to temperature, humidity, evaporation, and working time?
- What paste thickness is required after dipping and after drying?
- Does the material trap air easily?
- What cleaning method is needed for trays, plates, and contact surfaces?
- Is the paste compatible with the machine’s wetted materials and maintenance routine?
- What defects are currently observed: bridging, uneven coverage, voids, edge buildup, contamination, or paste fall-off?

A useful request is a process capability discussion based on your own acceptance limits. Ask the supplier to define the parameters that will be adjusted during trials, such as dip depth, paste level, dwell time, vacuum conditions, preheat profile, drying profile, transfer pressure, and carrier handling speed.

LONG lists the LGTM-6837 with adjustable paste-thickness settings in 0.001 mm increments, a stated paste-thickness precision of plus or minus 0.01 mm, and an integrated vacuum debubble function. Those specifications can be relevant to a process that needs controlled termination thickness and bubble removal. They should be treated as starting points for a sample trial, not as a substitute for qualification on the buyer’s material and product.

## Choose the Right Carrier Plate or Fixture Method

The carrier is part of the manufacturing process, not a minor accessory. It holds the components through loading, dipping, drying, transfer, and unloading. If the carrier does not match the component geometry or production flow, even a precise automatic dipping machine can suffer from misalignment, incomplete transfer, chipped parts, or inconsistent dipping depth.

Common holding methods include thin carrier plates, standard carrier plates, fiberglass JIG plates, stainless-steel plates, and specialized fixtures. The right choice depends on product size, geometry, heat exposure, required rigidity, cleaning needs, and how the part moves between process stages.

|Carrier or fixture consideration|Risk if it is poorly matched|Selection question|
|---|---|---|
|Hole size and positional tolerance|Parts can shift, tilt, jam, or receive uneven paste coverage.|Does the carrier pattern hold the component securely without applying excessive force?|
|Plate flatness and rigidity|Dipping depth can vary across the working area.|What flatness specification applies after repeated production cycles?|
|Material compatibility|Heat, paste, cleaning chemicals, or wear can shorten carrier life.|Is the plate material proven for the operating temperature and cleaning process?|
|Transfer method|Improper pressing or release can chip parts or damage fresh paste.|How are transfer force, alignment, and orientation controlled?|
|Changeover design|Frequent tooling changes can reduce uptime and introduce setup errors.|How many plates, conversions, and operator steps are required per product change?|

When comparing quotations, include carrier plates, press conversions, loading plates, cassettes, and other dedicated tooling in the total project scope. A lower-priced machine may not be lower cost if it requires extensive custom tooling, complex changeovers, or frequent replacement parts.

## Decide How Much Automatic Loading and Unloading You Need

Automation should match the production reality. A fully automatic line is valuable when volume, repeatability, labor availability, and traceability justify the investment. A semi-automatic solution can be a better fit for lower volumes, frequent product changes, or early-stage process development.

Consider the entire material flow, not just the dipping station:

1. How are components delivered to the machine?
2. How are they loaded into the carrier plate?
3. How are excess parts and debris removed?
4. How are carrier plates transferred through the wet process and oven?
5. How are finished parts unloaded and collected?
6. How are empty carriers cleaned, inspected, and returned?
7. What happens when the downstream process stops?

An automatic dipping machine can eliminate touchpoints only if upstream and downstream stages support the same pace. Otherwise, operators may simply move from one bottleneck to another.

For higher-volume passive-component production, automation features worth evaluating include automatic vibration loading, robotic or mechanical transport, servo-controlled pressing, magazine handling, in-line cleaning, buffer capacity, and automatic unloading. The best configuration protects the freshly dipped surface during transfer and provides a controlled response when a downstream fault occurs.

LONG’s LGTM-6837 product information describes an automated load-to-unload sequence that includes automatic chip shaking, debris removal, bottom insertion by servo press, preheating, vacuum dipping, vacuum debubbling, drying, cooling, transfer, unloading, and carrier cleaning. Its listed buffer is intended to maintain drying conditions when an abnormal condition interrupts normal operation. For manufacturers evaluating continuous production, this is the type of end-to-end flow that should be assessed, alongside real cycle time and recovery procedures.

## Calculate Required Output Using Good Units

Capacity claims need context. “Parts per hour” is useful only when the product size, carrier loading quantity, yield, machine availability, and operating schedule are all defined.

Use this basic calculation when estimating output:

`Required good parts per hour = Planned daily good output / Available productive hours per day`

Then work backward to account for expected yield, planned downtime, changeovers, cleaning, carrier handling, and preventive maintenance. This produces a more realistic required gross output than simply dividing monthly demand by calendar hours.

Ask every supplier to state capacity using the same conditions:

- Component type and dimensions
- Number of components per carrier
- Number of carriers processed per cycle
- Dipping, drying, and unloading cycle time
- Number of shifts and operating hours assumed
- Whether the rate is theoretical, demonstrated, or sustained
- Assumed yield and downtime exclusions
- Configuration of loading and unloading equipment

LONG cites an example capacity of approximately 1,100,000 pieces per hour for 0402 (1005 metric) chips on the LGTM-6837 product page, while a separate summary on that page references approximately 550,000 pieces per hour for 0402 parts. Because capacity depends on chip specification and line configuration, buyers should request a written capacity calculation for their exact product, process sequence, and acceptance criteria before using either figure in an investment model.

## Verify Precision, Quality Control, and Data Needs

Precision specifications are useful, but they must link directly to the quality characteristics that matter to your customer. A machine’s motion accuracy does not automatically prove final termination quality.

Build the factory acceptance test around measurable outcomes:

- Dipped-area dimensions and coverage
- Paste thickness and uniformity
- Void or bubble rate
- Part damage and chipping rate
- Part orientation and loading accuracy
- Cross-carrier and edge-to-center consistency
- Drying or curing result
- Electrical and adhesion test results, where applicable
- Repeatability over an agreed production run

Also consider how operators will run and maintain the line. Confirm recipe management, user access levels, alarm history, production counters, maintenance prompts, and any data-export or factory-integration requirements. A practical interface and clear fault recovery can matter more to daily output than an advanced function that operators cannot confidently use.

For regulated or high-reliability markets, align acceptance criteria with the customer’s applicable component, quality, and traceability requirements. The machine supplier should support the agreed factory acceptance test, document parameters used during the trial, and identify which process variables remain under the manufacturer’s control.

## Compare Total Cost of Ownership, Not Machine Price

The purchase price is only one part of the decision. The less visible costs can determine whether the investment pays back: tooling, carriers, utilities, paste waste, spare parts, training, maintenance, floor space, and the cost of quality losses during startup.

Use the following buyer checklist before releasing a purchase order:

- Confirm the quoted configuration includes all required loading, unloading, drying, and buffering equipment.
- Confirm which carrier plates, fixtures, press conversions, and cassettes are included.
- Review electrical power, compressed-air, exhaust, floor-space, weight, and installation requirements.
- Define the supplier’s commissioning, training, service, spare-parts, and remote-support scope.
- Agree on product samples, paste supply, test methods, and acceptance criteria before the trial.
- Compare changeover time and tooling cost for the full product mix, not only the highest-volume part.
- Request preventive-maintenance tasks, recommended spare-parts lists, and replacement lead times.
- Identify one accountable owner for process engineering, quality, maintenance, and purchasing decisions.

## FAQ: Choosing an Automatic Dipping Machine

**Q: Is a fully automatic dipping machine always better than a semi-automatic model?**
A: No. Full automation is generally most suitable when volume is stable, handling consistency is critical, and the upstream and downstream processes can support continuous flow. Semi-automatic equipment can be more practical for lower volumes, frequent product changes, or development work. The right choice depends on total process flow and cost per good part.

**Q: What information should I send to a dipping-machine supplier?**
A: Send component drawings or samples, size tolerances, dipping pattern, paste data sheet, carrier preference or current carrier drawings, required output, current defect data, factory utilities, layout limits, and quality acceptance criteria. This information allows a supplier to recommend a configuration rather than a generic model.

**Q: How should I validate a machine before purchase?**
A: Run a documented sample trial using the actual component and, where possible, the intended production paste. Agree on the process parameters, number of samples, inspection method, acceptance criteria, and required output. The trial should evaluate repeatability, not only whether one good sample can be made.

**Q: Can one automatic dipping machine run several product sizes?**
A: Often yes, but product range and changeover practicality must be verified. Different component sizes may require different carrier plates, press conversions, recipes, and loading settings. Ask for documented changeover steps, tooling costs, and a demonstration covering the smallest and largest parts in your expected product mix.

**Q: What is the most common procurement mistake?**
A: Selecting solely by theoretical parts-per-hour capacity or machine price. The better decision is based on qualified yield, product compatibility, material handling, changeover requirements, support capability, and the complete cost of ownership.
