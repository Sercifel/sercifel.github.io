# Glass Core Substrates and TGV: The Next Equipment Opportunity in Advanced Packaging
![](https://i.meee.com.tw/2oWDbco.png)

Advanced packaging is increasingly being asked to do work that once belonged mainly to transistor scaling. AI accelerators, high-performance computing systems, and chiplet architectures require larger packages, more interconnects, higher bandwidth, and tighter electrical design rules. That pressure is exposing some dimensional and warpage limits of conventional organic substrates. Glass core substrates are therefore receiving renewed attention—not as a universal replacement, but as a candidate for the most demanding package formats. Intel, for example, has identified glass substrates as a technology for future large, high-density packages, particularly in data-center, AI, and graphics applications.

For equipment suppliers, the change is significant. A glass core introduces new requirements for forming through-glass vias (TGVs), metallizing high-aspect-ratio openings, building fine redistribution layers, handling brittle panels, and detecting glass-specific defects. If glass advances from pilot lines into high-volume manufacturing, these process steps could create a distinct equipment cycle within advanced packaging.

## Why Glass Is Being Considered

Organic substrates remain the industry workhorse because their materials, fabrication infrastructure, and supply chains are mature. Their challenge is that very large, dense packages can experience dimensional change, warpage, and lithography alignment difficulties as layer counts and package sizes increase.

Glass offers a different property set. It can provide high dimensional stability, a smooth surface for fine-line routing, electrical insulation, relatively low high-frequency loss, and a coefficient of thermal expansion that can be tailored through composition. Intel has highlighted lower pattern distortion and improved flatness as reasons glass could support denser interconnects in future large packages. Fraunhofer IZM likewise identifies dimensional stability and smooth surfaces as important benefits for high-density redistribution layers.

These attributes are relevant to AI, HPC, graphics, and optical integration, where package size and I/O density are rising together. Yet glass is brittle, edge defects can propagate into cracks, and process flows must manage stress between glass, copper, dielectric films, and attached components. Industry research consequently treats glass as a promising but still emerging substrate platform rather than an immediate replacement for established organic technology.

## TGVs Are the Critical Enabling Process

A through-glass via is a vertical opening through the glass core that is subsequently metallized to carry signals, power, or ground between the two sides of the substrate. It plays a role similar to a through-silicon via, but the material and manufacturing economics differ.

TGV formation can use several approaches, including laser drilling and laser-induced modification followed by selective etching. Manufacturers need tight control of diameter, taper, sidewall quality, position, aspect ratio, and microcrack formation across a large substrate. Fraunhofer IZM reports flows using laser-based formation followed by sputtered or chemical seed layers and copper electroplating, showing that via formation is only the first stage of a longer integration sequence.

This is why TGV is better understood as a **process module** than as a single-tool opportunity.

## Where the Equipment Opportunity Appears

|Process area|Why glass changes the requirement|Relevant equipment categories|
|---|---|---|
|TGV formation|Dense holes require low damage and tight positional control|Laser modification/drilling, wet etch, cleaning|
|Via metallization|Narrow vias need continuous seed coverage and reliable copper deposition|PVD, electroless deposition, electrochemical plating|
|RDL buildup|Fine lines and large panels raise overlay and uniformity demands|Coating/lamination, lithography, develop/etch, plating|
|Inspection and metrology|Transparent, brittle substrates create new defect modes|AOI, CD/overlay metrology, crack inspection, warpage measurement|
|Handling and singulation|Panels must avoid edge damage and mechanical stress|Panel handling, carriers, edge treatment, precision singulation|

Inspection and metrology may be especially important because yield loss can originate early and become expensive after multiple buildup steps. SEMI’s TGV measurement guide addresses automated optical inspection of via geometry and alignment. Process-control development is also focusing on missing-via detection, microcracks, film thickness, overlay, and panel warpage. Transparent glass can require inspection approaches that differ from those used on opaque organic substrates.

Panel-level processing is another important variable. Glass can be produced in large formats, creating a potential cost advantage if lithography, wet processing, plating, and inspection remain uniform across the full panel. Fraunhofer’s Glass Panel Technology Group is explicitly working on scalable TGV and redistribution-layer processes, while industry development centers are combining panel lithography, coating, inspection, and metrology to shorten process-integration cycles.

## What Could Slow Adoption?

The equipment opportunity should not be confused with guaranteed near-term volume. Organic substrates continue to improve, and many advanced packages can remain economically attractive without changing core material. Glass also requires qualification of materials, interfaces, via reliability, edge integrity, assembly flows, and supply-chain standards.

High-volume economics will depend on yield as much as panel size. A larger panel creates more potential package area, but it can also amplify the cost of plating non-uniformity, lithography errors, cracks, or handling damage. Equipment suppliers therefore have to demonstrate repeatability, throughput, uptime, and process control—not simply technical feasibility.

Adoption is therefore likely to be staged: R&D and pilot demand first, followed by production tools where qualified glass-core platforms gain design wins. SEMI’s 2026 research continues to characterize glass core substrates as an emerging market whose growth depends on manufacturing development and adoption in AI, HPC, and other advanced applications.

## FAQ

**Q: What is a glass core substrate?**
A: It is a package substrate that uses glass as the structural core, with conductive vias and redistribution layers added to connect semiconductor dies to the package and board.

**Q: What is the difference between a TGV and a TSV?**
A: A TGV passes through glass, while a TSV passes through silicon. Glass is electrically insulating and can be processed in large panels; silicon has a mature semiconductor-processing base but different electrical and mechanical characteristics.

**Q: Why are AI and HPC driving interest in glass?**
A: These applications often require large packages with multiple chiplets and memory stacks. As package size and interconnect density rise, warpage control, fine-line routing, and signal integrity become more difficult.

**Q: Are glass core substrates already in high-volume production?**
A: TGV-based glass products already exist for applications such as RF, MEMS, sensors, and interposers. However, large glass-core package substrates for leading-edge AI and HPC remain broadly in development and qualification, with timelines varying by company and application.

**Q: Which equipment segments could benefit most?**
A: TGV formation, wet processing, metallization and copper plating, panel lithography, inspection/metrology, and specialized handling are the most directly exposed categories.

**Q: What is the biggest manufacturing risk?**
A: Yield. Microcracks, via defects, metallization voids, overlay errors, warpage, contamination, and edge damage can all reduce output as panel size and interconnect density increase.

## Conclusion

Glass core substrates are gaining attention because advanced packaging is moving toward larger, denser, and more electrically demanding systems. TGVs make those substrates functional, but they create manufacturing challenges spanning glass structuring, metallization, RDL formation, inspection, and handling.

For equipment suppliers, glass is therefore more than a materials transition; it may become a process-infrastructure transition. The strongest opportunities are likely to emerge where existing semiconductor and panel-processing tools must be adapted for brittle, transparent, large-format glass. The pace of that opportunity will ultimately depend on qualification, yield, standardization, and the economics of volume production.
